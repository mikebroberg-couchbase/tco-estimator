Methodology & validation notes

Cost model. For each selected tool: infrastructure cost = $/ops-sec/yr × ops/sec, summed across every tool you select. Team & integration overhead is computed once for the whole stack — not per tool, then summed — and added to that infrastructure sum to get the current stack total (see "Why team overhead scales..." below). Couchbase is modeled the same way: its own infrastructure cost, plus its own single-platform overhead. The gap between the stack's overhead and Couchbase's overhead, not a discount applied to any competitor's infrastructure price, is the source of the savings estimate.

Not every vendor is priced the same way. Databases don't all sell by the same yardstick. Some charge per operation, some rent you a server by the hour, and one vendor's number comes directly from a benchmark that vendor published themselves. This tool prices each vendor by whichever method that vendor actually sells through — not by picking whichever framing produces a smaller number. When a vendor rents a server, the list price gets divided by a reasonable estimate of how much traffic that server can handle. That traffic estimate is ours, not the vendor's, and it's disclosed on every row that uses it.

Watch for a cheaper tier that doesn't apply here. A few vendors sell a separate, cheaper tier meant for small or bursty traffic — a dev/test tier, or pay-as-you-go pricing with no minimum commitment. Those tiers are real, but they're not what this tool models, because they don't hold up at the steady, production-scale traffic this calculator assumes.

Complexity reduction. Reduction = 1 − (1 system after ÷ N systems selected today). Selecting a single tool is a 1-for-1 swap with nothing to consolidate, so it correctly shows 0%; the reduction grows as more systems are replaced by the one consolidated Couchbase platform (2 tools → 50%, 3 → 67%, 5 → 80%). It's directional — meant to make the qualitative case (less duplication, smaller footprint, fewer integration points), not a formal risk score.

Why team overhead scales with how much you consolidate, not just what you pick. Running one more database doesn't need a whole additional administrator — real teams share tooling, monitoring, and process across the systems they already run. But every additional disparate system you keep also adds real integration work: more point-to-point glue code, more places data has to be kept in sync. This tool reflects both effects: the team-overhead line grows slower than 1-for-1 as you add tools (shared admin efficiency), while the integration burden grows faster than 1-for-1 (more systems to keep talking to each other) — and a single tool with nothing to integrate with costs nothing extra on that front. If you select just one tool, this tool shows zero assumed labor savings, because no consolidation has actually happened yet — any savings shown in that case come from infrastructure pricing alone, not an assumed efficiency gain.

Ranges, not points. Headline numbers are shown as a range (roughly ±12–15%) because even a precisely-sourced vendor price is being multiplied by a modeled workload — real traffic has spikes and quiet periods that a single number can't capture. Unlocking Step 3 lets you tighten the range with your own actual contracts and traffic patterns.

Reality check. Every default figure in this tool is sourced to a vendor's own public pricing page, a vendor's own published benchmark, or — for the handful of rows with no public price at all — a clearly labeled estimate. Two things remain our own modeling choices, not the vendor's, and both are disclosed rather than hidden: how much traffic a rented server can realistically handle, and which of a vendor's pricing tiers matches the steady, ongoing production workload this tool is built to model.

Inference savings — a separate model. The inference card estimates what caching takes off your model bill: annual savings = annual model spend × addressable share × cache hit rate, shown as a range driven by a low and high hit rate. It is reported as its own line item and is never added to the consolidation savings figure or its denominator. That separation is deliberate: at mid-market model spend, inference frequently costs more per year than the entire database stack, so blending the two would let an inference assumption swamp the consolidation result that is the actual subject of this tool.

Why hit rate is the whole answer. Cache savings reduce to how often a request can be served without calling the model. That rate is a property of the workload, not of Couchbase — a bounded FAQ assistant and an open-ended agent differ by roughly an order of magnitude. Whatever value is assumed for it effectively is the output, which is why it is exposed as a named archetype with a visible range rather than buried as a constant.
